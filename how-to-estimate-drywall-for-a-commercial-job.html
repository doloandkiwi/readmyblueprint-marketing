How to estimate drywall for a commercial job

By The ReadMyBlueprint Team · Oct 28, 2026 · Updated Oct 28, 2026

To estimate drywall for a commercial job, total the linear feet of each wall type, multiply by wall height and by the number of faces and layers, add ceiling area, divide by the area of the sheet size you will hang (32 sq ft for 4x8, 40 for 4x10, 48 for 4x12), then add waste. Do it per wall type, not for the whole building, because commercial plans mix rated and unrated assemblies with different board, layers and heights.

Step 1: Work by wall type, not by room

Commercial sets carry a wall-type legend (often tags like A1, B2, C3). Each tag defines stud size, spacing, board type, number of layers per side and fire rating. Measure the linear feet of each tag separately. A corridor wall rated for one hour and a tenant demising wall with two layers each side need different board counts even at the same length. If the legend is unclear, see how to read a wall type legend.

Step 2: Wall area per type

Wall area = linear feet x height x faces x layers. Most partitions have two faces. A shaft wall or a one-sided furring wall has one. Use the height from the wall-type detail: full height to deck is common in commercial work, and it is usually taller than the ceiling height in the room.

Step 3: Openings

Practice varies. Many estimators deduct only large openings and let small door openings stand as a built-in allowance for cuts and waste. Pick one rule, write it into your bid notes, and use it every time. Do not deduct and also add heavy waste for the same openings.

Step 4: Ceilings and soffits

Gypsum board ceilings are measured in square feet from the reflected ceiling plan, not the floor plan. Add bulkheads, soffits and returns separately. Acoustic tile ceilings use no board at all, so check the finish schedule before counting.

Step 5: Convert to sheets

  • 4x8 sheet = 32 sq ft
  • 4x10 sheet = 40 sq ft
  • 4x12 sheet = 48 sq ft

Choose the length that gives the fewest butt joints on your wall height. Longer boards mean fewer joints to finish but heavier handling and tighter delivery access. Round up to whole sheets per board type, since type X, moisture-resistant and standard board are ordered separately.

Step 6: Waste

Typical drywall waste allowances run roughly 5 to 15 percent, higher on cut-up layouts with many short walls, curves and ceilings, lower on long straight corridors. Use the figure your own jobs have shown, not a rule from a website. We do not publish a universal waste number because it depends on layout and crew.

Worked example

Assumptions: 400 LF of single-layer unrated partition, 10 ft tall, both faces. 150 LF of rated partition, 10 ft tall, two layers of type X each face. 3,000 sq ft of board ceiling. 10 percent waste. No opening deductions.

  • Unrated walls: 400 x 10 x 2 x 1 = 8,000 sq ft. At 40 sq ft per 4x10 sheet = 200 sheets. With 10 percent waste = 220 sheets.
  • Rated walls: 150 x 10 x 2 x 2 = 6,000 sq ft of type X. 6,000 / 40 = 150 sheets. With 10 percent waste = 165 sheets.
  • Ceiling: 3,000 / 32 = 93.75, so 94 sheets of 4x8. With 10 percent waste = 103 sheets.

Total: 220 standard, 165 type X, 103 ceiling sheets. Screws, tape, compound, corner bead and labor are separate lines. Those depend on your finish level and your own production rates, which this page does not cover.

Where commercial estimates go wrong: counting a rated assembly as one layer, using ceiling height instead of deck height, missing walls that appear only on an enlarged plan, and mixing wall types into one total.

Frequently asked questions

How do you calculate drywall for a commercial building?

Total the linear feet of each wall type, multiply by height, faces and layers, add ceiling area, divide by sheet area (32, 40 or 48 sq ft), then add waste.

How much waste should I add to a commercial drywall estimate?

Typical allowances run roughly 5 to 15 percent depending on layout. Use the figure your own jobs have shown.

Do fire-rated walls change the sheet count?

Yes. Rated assemblies often use two layers of type X on each face, which doubles the board area compared with a single layer.
