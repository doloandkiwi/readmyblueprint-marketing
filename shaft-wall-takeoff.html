Shaft wall takeoff: liner panel, C-H studs, J-track and face layers

By The ReadMyBlueprint Team · Nov 18, 2026

Short answer: take off a shaft wall as its own assembly, never as a partition. Measure linear feet around each shaft and use slab-to-slab height, then count four things separately: liner panels (by panel width), C-H studs (about one fewer than panels per run), J-runner (top, bottom and at each end of a run) and face board area per layer on the room side. Repeat per floor, because shafts run through every level.

This guide covers the common cavity shaft wall system: thick gypsum liner panels on the shaft side, held by C-H studs (some systems use C-T, I or E shaped studs) in J-shaped runners, with Type X board on the side you can reach. Manufacturer names and dimensions vary. The system named in your spec and the tested design on the wall type govern; this page is the counting method.

Why shaft wall gets missed or miscounted

  • It looks like any other wall in plan. On the floor plan a shaft wall is two lines with a tag. You find it from the tag, the core plans and the shaft sections, not from the drawing style.
  • It runs slab to slab. Room partitions may stop at the ceiling or a few inches above it. Shaft wall closes the shaft floor to floor, so ceiling height undercounts every floor.
  • It shows up on every level. An elevator shaft in a four-story building is four floors of shaft wall plus whatever the sections show at the pit and the overrun. Count per floor, then add.
  • It hides in more than elevators. Stair enclosures, duct shafts, trash and linen chutes and plumbing chases can all use the same system. Search the legend for every wall type that names a shaft wall system.
  • It gets folded into partitions. Liner panel, C-H studs and J-runner are different materials from standard board, stud and track. If they sit on one line they get priced like a partition.

Where the shaft wall is written in the plans

  • Wall-type legend or partition schedule. The shaft wall type lists the liner panel, stud depth, layers on the room side and a tested design number with an hour rating. See how to read a wall type legend.
  • Core plans and enlarged plans. Elevator and stair cores are often drawn at a larger scale on their own sheets with the shaft walls tagged.
  • Building and wall sections. These give floor-to-floor heights, the pit depth and the overrun at the top of the shaft.
  • Details. Head-of-wall, base and door-frame details tell you what happens at the slab, at deflection joints and around elevator doors.
  • Specifications. The gypsum board assemblies section, and often a separate shaft wall paragraph, names the system and any substitution rules.

What to count, line by line

  • Liner panels (each). Count by panel width along each wall run. Panels are commonly 24 in. wide and about 1 in. thick, cut to height. A 9 ft run at 24 in. is 4.5 panels, so 5 positions. Count positions, not area, because a cut panel is still a panel bought.
  • C-H studs (each, by length). One between each pair of panels in a run, so panels minus one per straight run. Length is the slab-to-slab height. Depth and gauge come from the wall type and the system's height tables.
  • J-runner (linear feet). Top and bottom of every run, plus a vertical J at each end of a run and at corners, as the system's corner detail shows. Some systems use E-studs or a different end condition; follow the detail.
  • Face board (square feet per layer, then sheets). Measure on the room side, where the board goes. Multiply area by the number of layers the wall type calls for. Convert to sheets as you would any Type X board.
  • Openings and extras. Elevator door openings, access panels and duct penetrations each get a count. Framing, rated head details and firestopping at penetrations are often in the drywall scope; read the spec for who supplies the door frame.
  • Finishing. Only the room side is finished. Assign it a finish level like any other wall (see drywall finish levels); the shaft side gets none.

Worked example: one elevator shaft, four floors

The job (example inputs, not a real project): one elevator shaft, 8 ft by 9 ft, four floors, 12 ft slab to slab. The wall type calls for the cavity shaft wall system with 24 in. liner panels and two layers of 5/8 in. Type X on the corridor side. One elevator door per floor, 42 in. by 84 in. For simple arithmetic we measure the face side along the same 34 LF; on a real job measure it on the room-side line.
Length per floor: 8 + 9 + 8 + 9 = 34 LF. Wall area: 34 x 12 = 408 sq ft.
Liner panels per floor: 8 ft run = 4 panels, 9 ft run = 4.5, so 5. Total 4 + 5 + 4 + 5 = 18 panels at 12 ft.
C-H studs per floor: panels minus one per run: 3 + 4 + 3 + 4 = 14 studs at 12 ft.
J-runner per floor: top and bottom 34 x 2 = 68 LF. Vertical J at both ends of each of the 4 runs: 8 x 12 = 96 LF. Total 164 LF.
Face board per floor: door 3.5 x 7 = 24.5 sq ft. Net 408 - 24.5 = 383.5 sq ft per layer, x 2 layers = 767 sq ft.
Four floors: 72 liner panels, 56 C-H studs, 656 LF of J-runner, and 767 x 4 = 3,068 sq ft of face board. In 4x12 sheets (48 sq ft): 3,068 / 48 = 63.9, so 64 sheets before waste. With a 10% waste factor as an example input, 64 x 1.10 = 70.4, so 71 sheets.
If you had used ceiling height instead: at a 9 ft ceiling the face board would be (34 x 9 - 24.5) x 2 x 4 = 2,252 sq ft, which is 816 sq ft short, and every liner panel and stud would be 3 ft short.

Two things to check on a real set. First, the pit and the overrun: the sections often show shaft wall below the lowest floor and above the top floor, which adds height the floor plans never show. Second, not every side of a shaft is always shaft wall. A side that backs onto a stair or a concrete wall may be a different type, so tag each run before you count it. Pick your waste factor from your own history; our drywall waste factor guide covers how.

Mistakes that cost money

  • Pricing shaft wall as a rated partition. Different materials, installed from one side. Keep it as its own assembly.
  • Ceiling height instead of slab to slab. The example above loses a quarter of the height on every floor.
  • Counting liner by area. Liner is bought by panel. Area divided by panel size drops the cut pieces at the ends of each run.
  • Missing the small shafts. Duct and chute shafts are short runs on many floors. They add up and they carry the same rating.
  • Skipping the head and door details. Deflection heads and elevator door framing are labor and material that never show on the floor plan.

Shaft wall takeoff FAQ

What is a shaft wall?

A shaft wall is a fire-rated gypsum wall built around an elevator, stair, duct or chase shaft from one side only, the side outside the shaft. The common cavity system uses thick gypsum liner panels set between C-H or similar studs held in J-shaped runners, with one or more layers of Type X board on the room side.

How do you do a shaft wall takeoff?

Find every shaft on the core plans and shaft sections, read the wall type and its tested design for the layer count, then measure linear feet and slab-to-slab height per shaft per floor. From that, count liner panels by panel width, C-H studs between panels, J-runner top, bottom and at ends, and face board area per layer on the room side. Keep each item on its own line.

How many C-H studs does a shaft wall need?

In the common cavity system each liner panel sits between two studs, so a straight run needs roughly one stud fewer than the number of panels, with J-runner at the two ends. A run that takes 5 panels takes 4 studs. Stud spacing comes from the panel width, commonly 24 inches, but check the system and the height limits in the manufacturer's tables for your stud depth and gauge.

Is shaft wall height measured to the ceiling or to the slab?

To the slab or structure above. A shaft wall has to close the shaft from floor to floor to keep its rating, so use the floor-to-floor height less the slab, not the ceiling height in the room. Using ceiling height is one of the most common ways shaft wall gets undercounted.

Do you deduct the elevator door opening from a shaft wall?

Deduct it from the face board area if that is your rule for openings of that size, and note it. Liner panels are usually counted by panel position and cut around the opening, so the panel count rarely drops. The door frame itself is often supplied by the elevator or door contractor, but the framing and the rated head detail around it are usually in the drywall scope, so read the spec.

Is a shaft wall the same as a fire-rated partition?

No. Both are rated, but a regular rated partition is built from both sides with studs and board on each face. A shaft wall is installed from one side because the shaft side is not reachable, and it uses a different system: liner panels, C-H or similar studs and J-runner. Price them as separate assemblies.

Can takeoff software count shaft wall?

It can give you the linear feet and area for walls tagged with the shaft wall type.
