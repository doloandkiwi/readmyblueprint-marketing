Per man-hour, per piece or per square foot? How drywall and steel stud subs price a bid

By The ReadMyBlueprint Team · Nov 17, 2026 · 8 min read

The short answer: price per man-hour when the scope is loose or the work is time and material, per piece when walls are long, repeated and measured, and per square foot when you have a proven rate for that exact kind of surface. All three methods start from the same quantity takeoff, so a wrong count breaks every one of them.

These questions come up in the Facebook groups for drywall and steel stud contractors: whether to bid steel stud framing per man-hour or per piece, whether a per-square-foot price for L4 and L5 finish holds up, and how to estimate steel stud labor and material on commercial jobs. The answers there vary because the right method depends on the job. This post lays out each method, what it costs you in risk, and a short worked example. The numbers in the example are made up for illustration. They are not market rates, so use your own production history.

Every method starts with the same counts

Before you choose a pricing method, you need the quantities:

  • Linear feet of wall by type, read from the plan and the wall type legend. If you are missing the legend, see what to do when there is no wall type legend.
  • Studs and track for each wall type, from height and spacing. Our metal stud calculation guide and metal stud calculator show the arithmetic.
  • Board sheets and square feet, by side and by layer, since a wall with two layers on one face is not the same as one layer each side.
  • Finish level by area, because the finish you price changes the hours. See drywall finish levels.

A man-hour bid uses these to size the hours. A per-piece bid multiplies them by a unit price. A square-foot bid divides the total by the board area. In all three cases an undercounted wall becomes a loss you only see after the job is awarded.

Per man-hour

You estimate the hours each task takes (layout, track, studs, hang, tape, finish) from your own past jobs, then multiply by your loaded labor cost and add material.

  • Good for: time and material work, change orders, repairs, jobs with odd layouts, and anything where the scope may move. It is the most honest method when you cannot see all the conditions.
  • Watch out for: it depends fully on your hour estimates. Optimistic hours are the usual way to lose money, and a crew that works slower than your history will eat the margin. It is also harder to compare against other bids.

Per piece (per board, per stud, per linear foot of track)

You price each unit installed: a unit price per sheet of board hung, per stud set, or per linear foot of track. The price already contains the labor, so the counts do most of the work.

  • Good for: tract and repeat work, long straight walls, and commercial jobs with large, clean quantities where you have solid unit history. It also makes it easy to pay crews by the piece.
  • Watch out for: unit prices hide difficulty. A stud in a long clean run costs less to set than one in a short wall with openings, so the same unit price over- or under-pays different areas. High ceilings, stair walls and soffits need their own adjustments.

Per square foot

You price each square foot of board surface, often split by finish level. It is the quick way to compare a bid against a budget and the form many GCs expect.

  • Good for: early budgets, tract homes and simple rooms, and finish work where area is a fair proxy for effort.
  • Watch out for: the rate only holds if the surface matches the work your rate came from. Floor SF is not wall SF. An L5 skim coat is not an L4. Short walls, soffits and tall rooms push labor per square foot up. If you get a per-square-foot price from a competitor, you cannot see what is inside it.

A worked example, with made-up numbers

Illustration only: every rate below is a placeholder to show the arithmetic. They are not market rates and not ours. Replace them with your own production history.

Say the plans show 400 linear feet of one wall type: 3-5/8 inch steel studs at 16 inches on center, 10 feet tall, one layer of 5/8 inch board on each side.

  • Studs: 400 ft x 12 / 16 = 300 stud spaces, plus one to close the run, so about 301. Corners and openings add more, and you count those separately.
  • Track: top and bottom, 400 ft x 2 = 800 linear feet.
  • Board area: 400 ft x 10 ft x 2 sides = 8,000 SF. In 4x12 sheets of 48 SF each, that is about 167 sheets before waste.

Now price the same quantity three ways, using placeholder rates:

  • Man-hour: assume your history says 40 SF of board per labor hour, hang and finish together. 8,000 / 40 = 200 hours. At a placeholder $50 per hour, that is $10,000.
  • Per piece: at a placeholder $60 per sheet installed, 167 sheets = $10,020.
  • Per square foot: at a placeholder $1.25 per SF of board surface, 8,000 SF = $10,000.

The three land close together only because we built the example that way. In a real bid they will differ, and the gap is information. If your per-hour figure comes out well above the per-square-foot figure, check whether the job has short walls, height or access problems that a flat rate misses. Pricing one job two ways and comparing is a cheap check.

Now change the count. If 40 of those 400 feet were missed, which is 10 percent of the wall, all three totals drop by about 10 percent without anyone noticing. The pricing method was never the problem. The count was.

Which method fits which job

  • Tract or repeat work: per piece or per square foot, with unit rates from your own finished jobs.
  • Commercial jobs with large clean quantities: per piece or per linear foot of wall by type, checked against man-hours.
  • Commercial jobs with many short walls, soffits and bulkheads: man-hours for those areas, or separate wall types with their own rates.
  • Time and material and change orders: man-hours, with written records of hours and materials.
  • Finish-only scopes: per square foot by finish level, applied to the wall area that actually gets that finish.

Questions estimators ask

Should I bid steel stud framing per man-hour or per piece? Use man-hours when the scope is uncertain, the work is time and material, or the layout is cut up with many short walls and soffits. Use per-piece (per stud or per linear foot of track) when walls are long, repetitive and well defined, and you have your own production history to back the unit price. Whichever you use, check it against the other method before you submit.

Is price per square foot for L4 and L5 finish a fair way to bid? It works when you apply it to the right square footage. The usual mistake is mixing wall-face SF with floor SF, or applying one rate to areas that need different levels of finish. Count board SF by wall type and by finish level, then apply your own historical rate for each. A Level 5 surface needs more labor and material than a Level 4, so one blended rate across both will be wrong on one of them.

How do I estimate steel stud labor and material on a commercial job? Start with linear feet of wall by type from the plans, then convert to studs, track, board and fasteners using each wall's height and spacing. Price material from those counts with waste added. For labor, apply your own production rates per stud, per linear foot or per hour, then adjust for height, access, layout complexity and schedule.

What production rate should I use for hanging and finishing drywall? Use your own. Production depends on crew, board size, ceiling height, finish level and how clean the site is, so a number from a forum will not match your crews. Pull hours and quantities from your last few finished jobs of the same type and average them. We do not publish production rates and have not checked anyone else's.

When should I use time and material instead of a fixed price? Use time and material for change orders with unclear scope, repairs, patching and work where you cannot see the conditions until you open them. Use a fixed price when the scope is drawn and measurable. Keep a written record of hours and materials either way so a change order has support.

How do I stop underbidding when the plans have lots of short walls and soffits? Short walls and soffits take more labor per square foot than long clean runs, so a flat square-foot rate underprices them. Count them as their own wall types, or switch to man-hours for those areas. Check the quantity first: short or partial walls are where missed or double-counted footage tends to hide.

Can takeoff software give me the quantities for any of these pricing methods? Yes. Any of the three methods needs the same quantities: linear feet of wall by type, studs, track, board sheets and square feet. Software can produce those counts, and you then apply your own rates. You still review the counts before you bid.
