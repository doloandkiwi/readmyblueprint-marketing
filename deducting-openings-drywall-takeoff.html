Do you deduct doors and windows in a drywall takeoff?

By The ReadMyBlueprint Team · Nov 25, 2026

Short answer: it depends on what you are counting, so keep two numbers. For board (sheets), most estimators deduct only large openings and leave small ones in, because the piece cut out of a door opening is rarely reusable. For finish area (tape, mud, paint), deduct every opening, because nobody finishes a hole. Then add back the jamb returns and count the corner bead around each opening. Write the size threshold down and use the same one on every bid.

The threshold is a company rule, not a code. A common one is "deduct any opening bigger than one sheet," which is 32 sq ft for a 4x8. Use your own history to set it. What matters is that you never leave small openings in and also add a heavy waste factor for the same cut-outs, because then you price that waste twice.

Why one rule for everything gets the number wrong

  • Board and finish are bought differently. Sheets are bought whole. A 3 ft by 7 ft door opening is cut out of a full sheet, and the cut-out is usually scrap. Finish labor and paint follow the surface that actually exists.
  • Deducting a small opening leaves the cut-out unpaid. If you deduct a 21 sq ft door from the sheet count, you have counted the board as if it was never bought. On a floor with many doors that adds up to sheets you will not have.
  • Not deducting a big opening buys board for a hole. A storefront, a wide cased opening or a run of windows can be several sheets each. Leaving those in pads the bid.
  • Openings add work around the edges. Window and cased-opening returns need board, and every outside edge needs corner bead or trim bead. A deduction that ignores this undercounts the edges.

Where the openings are in the plans

  • Door schedule. Width and height per door mark. Use the frame or rough opening size, not the door leaf, for the hole in the board.
  • Window schedule and elevations. Window sizes and head heights. Exterior elevations show which windows sit in walls you are boarding.
  • Floor plans. Cased openings and pass-throughs often have no schedule. Read the width off the plan and the height from a section, an interior elevation or a note.
  • Details. Jamb and head details show the return depth and whether the edge gets corner bead, J-bead or L-bead against a frame.
  • Wall type. Tells you if both faces get board. A partition opening removes area from both faces; an exterior wall only from the inside face. See how to read a wall type legend.

A rule you can write down

  • Board count: start from gross wall area. Deduct openings over your threshold (example: 32 sq ft). Leave smaller ones in as the allowance for their cut-outs.
  • Finish area: deduct all openings, any size.
  • Returns: add the jamb returns (opening perimeter times return depth) to both board and finish area when the detail shows board wrapping into the opening.
  • Edges: count corner bead or trim bead in linear feet around each opening, per face.
  • Waste: apply your normal waste factor after this. Do not raise it to cover openings you already left in. Our drywall waste factor guide covers how to set it.

Worked example: one classroom, three ways

The room (example inputs, not a real project): 30 ft by 24 ft, 9 ft walls, board on the inside face only. Two doors at 3 ft x 7 ft, four windows at 6 ft x 6 ft, one cased opening at 8 ft wide x 8 ft high. Window and cased-opening returns are 6 in. deep and boarded. Threshold: deduct openings over 32 sq ft. Sheets are 4x12 (48 sq ft). Waste factor 10% as an example input.
Gross wall area: perimeter 30 + 24 + 30 + 24 = 108 LF, x 9 = 972 sq ft.
Openings: doors 2 x 21 = 42 sq ft (each under 32, left in). Windows 4 x 36 = 144 sq ft (each over 32, deducted). Cased opening 64 sq ft (deducted). All openings total 250 sq ft.
Returns: each window perimeter is 24 LF, x 0.5 ft = 12 sq ft, x 4 = 48 sq ft. Cased opening sides and head 8 + 8 + 8 = 24 LF, x 0.5 = 12 sq ft. Total returns 60 sq ft.
Board, with the rule: 972 - 144 - 64 + 60 = 824 sq ft. 824 / 48 = 17.2, so 18 sheets. With 10% waste, 18 x 1.10 = 19.8, so 20 sheets.
Board, deducting everything: 972 - 250 + 60 = 782 sq ft. 782 / 48 = 16.3, so 17 sheets, 19 with waste. The two door cut-outs are now unpaid.
Board, deducting nothing: 972 + 60 = 1,032 sq ft. 1,032 / 48 = 21.5, so 22 sheets, 25 with waste. Five more sheets than the rule, mostly for window holes.
Finish area (tape, mud, paint): deduct all openings: 972 - 250 + 60 = 782 sq ft.
Corner bead: windows 4 x 24 = 96 LF. The cased opening is open on both sides, so 24 LF on each face = 48 LF. Total 144 LF, before your own allowance for laps.

Same room, three board counts: 19, 20 or 25 sheets with waste. The rule you pick moves the number, which is why it has to be written down and used the same way on every bid. On a real set, check the door schedule for frame sizes and the jamb detail for whether a window return is board or a factory-finished liner, because a liner removes the return from your count.

Mistakes that cost money

  • Leaving small openings in and padding waste for them. You price the same cut-outs twice. Pick one.
  • Using the door leaf size. The hole in the board is the frame or rough opening, a little bigger than the leaf.
  • Deducting a partition opening from one face only. An opening through a two-sided partition removes board on both faces, and needs bead on both.
  • Forgetting returns and bead. A deduction without the returns undercounts board, and the bead is labor you did not price.
  • Missing the openings with no schedule. Cased openings, pass-throughs and borrowed lights live on the floor plan and in elevations only.
  • Changing the rule job to job. Your bids stop being comparable and your waste history stops meaning anything.

Deducting openings FAQ

Do you deduct doors and windows when estimating drywall?

Keep two numbers. For the sheet count, most estimators deduct only large openings and leave small ones such as standard doors in, because the cut-out piece is usually scrap. For finish area (tape, mud and paint), deduct every opening. Then add back boarded jamb returns and count corner bead around each opening.

What size opening should you deduct from a drywall sheet count?

There is no code for it; it is a company rule. A common one is to deduct any opening bigger than one sheet, which is 32 sq ft for a 4x8 sheet. Pick a threshold from your own job history, write it down and use the same one on every bid.

Should openings be deducted twice on a partition?

Yes, if the partition has board on both faces. An opening through a two-sided wall removes board from both faces and needs bead on both edges. An exterior wall with board on the inside only loses area from one face.

Do you add drywall back for window returns?

When the jamb detail shows board wrapping into the opening, yes. Multiply the opening perimeter (or sides and head for a door-height opening) by the return depth and add it to both board and finish area. If the window has a factory-finished liner or a wood jamb, there is no board return to count.

Is it double counting to leave openings in and add a waste factor?

It can be. Leaving small openings in is itself an allowance for their cut-outs. If you also raise your waste factor to cover the same cut-outs, you price that waste twice. Use your normal waste factor and do not inflate it for openings you already left in.

Do you measure the door leaf or the frame?

Use the frame or rough opening size, which is what is actually cut out of the board. The door leaf is smaller and understates the hole. The door schedule usually gives the leaf size, so check the frame type and jamb detail.

Can takeoff software deduct openings for me?
