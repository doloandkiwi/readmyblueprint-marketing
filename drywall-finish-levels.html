Drywall finish levels 0 to 5: what each one means for your takeoff

By The ReadMyBlueprint Team · Nov 11, 2026

Short answer: there are six drywall finish levels, 0 to 5. Level 0 is bare board, Level 1 is tape only, Level 2 is a thin coat over the tape, Level 3 is ready for texture, Level 4 is ready for flat paint, and Level 5 is Level 4 plus a skim coat over the whole surface. The level does not change your sheet count. It changes joint compound, coats and finishing labor, so it needs its own area column on the takeoff.

The levels come from the Gypsum Association document GA-214, Recommended Levels of Finish for Gypsum Board, Glass Mat and Fiber-Reinforced Gypsum Panels, and are also referenced in ASTM C840. The summary below is in our own words. On a real job, the wording in the current edition and in your project spec is what governs.

The six levels in plain English

  • Level 0. Board is hung. No tape, no compound, no accessories finished. Used for temporary construction or where the final finish has not been decided.
  • Level 1. Tape is set in joint compound at joints and interior angles. Tool marks and ridges are acceptable. Used where the surface is hidden from view: above ceilings, in plenums, attics and service corridors. Often called fire taping.
  • Level 2. Tape is embedded and wiped down, leaving a thin coat over joints and angles. Fastener heads and accessories get one coat. Tool marks are acceptable. Commonly specified where gypsum panels sit behind tile, and in garages and storage areas.
  • Level 3. One more coat over joints and angles, and two coats over fasteners and accessories. The compound is smooth and free of tool marks. Meant for surfaces that get a medium or heavy texture or a heavy wallcovering.
  • Level 4. Two separate coats over flat joints, one over interior angles, and three over fasteners and accessories, all smooth. The common level for flat paint, light texture and light wallcovering.
  • Level 5. Everything in Level 4, plus a thin skim coat of joint compound, or a product made for the purpose, over the entire surface. Called for under gloss, semi-gloss and enamel paints and where strong side lighting would show the joints.

What the level changes on a takeoff, and what it does not

  • Does not change: board area, sheet count, board type, framing, screws for hanging.
  • Changes: number of compound coats, compound quantity, sanding, and finishing labor. Level 5 adds a coat over the full board area, not only along the joints, so it scales with square feet of surface instead of linear feet of joint.
  • Often changes with it: primer and paint scope. A Level 5 wall is usually there because of the paint or the lighting, so read the painting section alongside the gypsum board section.

We do not list prices or production rates per level here. They vary by crew, region and job, and the published ranges we have seen do not say where their numbers come from. Use your own job history, and get the areas right first.

Where the finish level is written

  • The specifications. The gypsum board section, numbered 09 29 00 in MasterFormat, normally has a finishing paragraph that assigns a level to each kind of location.
  • The room finish schedule. Some sets add a finish-level column or a note per room, especially for lobbies and feature walls.
  • General notes and partition type sheets. Look for notes such as how far above the ceiling the finish must run, and what happens above the ceiling on rated walls.
  • The painting section. Gloss levels and specialty coatings there can require a higher board finish than the default.

If the set gives no level, or the drawings and spec disagree, write an RFI. Guessing Level 4 everywhere is the most common way a finishing number goes wrong in either direction.

Worked example: splitting one floor by finish level

The job: an office fit-out with 300 LF of partition, board on both sides, running 12 ft to the deck. The suspended ceiling is at 9 ft. Openings are ignored here to keep the arithmetic visible; deduct them by your own rule.
Total board area: 300 x 12 x 2 sides = 7,200 sq ft. This is what the sheet count comes from, at any level.
Above the ceiling (Level 1): 300 x 3 x 2 = 1,800 sq ft.
Below the ceiling: 300 x 9 x 2 = 5,400 sq ft, split three ways:
  Restroom walls behind tile (Level 2): 30 LF x 9 ft, one side = 270 sq ft.
  Reception walls under wall-washer lighting (Level 5): 40 LF x 9 ft, one side = 360 sq ft.
  Everything else, flat paint (Level 4): 5,400 - 270 - 360 = 4,770 sq ft.
Check: 1,800 + 270 + 360 + 4,770 = 7,200 sq ft. The level columns add back to the board total, so nothing was counted twice or dropped.
Add the ceiling: a 12 x 20 ft gypsum board ceiling in reception at Level 5 adds 240 sq ft, so Level 5 totals 360 + 240 = 600 sq ft.

Two things to notice. A quarter of the board on this job (1,800 of 7,200 sq ft) never gets more than tape, and pricing all of it at Level 4 overstates the finishing. And the Level 5 area is small but lands on the surfaces the owner looks at first. Many specs also ask for the finish to run a few inches above the ceiling line; if yours does, move that strip from the Level 1 column to the level below it.

Mistakes that cost money

  • One level for the whole job. Almost every commercial set has at least two levels once you count the space above the ceiling.
  • Missing a Level 5 note. It is often one line in the spec or one tag on a finish schedule, and it applies to full surface area.
  • Treating rated walls like any other wall above the ceiling. Joint treatment on a rated partition follows the tested assembly.
  • Forgetting ceilings and soffits. Gypsum board ceilings usually carry the same level as the walls in the room, and they catch side light more than walls do.
  • Mixing up board behind tile. Gypsum panels and cement backer units are finished differently. Read the wall type.

Drywall finish levels FAQ

What are the levels of drywall finish?

There are six, numbered 0 to 5, set out in the Gypsum Association's GA-214 and referenced in ASTM C840. Level 0 is board hung with no taping. Level 1 is tape set in compound. Level 2 adds a thin coat over the tape and one coat on fasteners. Level 3 adds another coat and is meant for texture. Level 4 is the usual level for flat paint. Level 5 is Level 4 plus a skim coat over the whole surface.

What is the difference between a Level 4 and a Level 5 finish?

Level 5 is a Level 4 finish with a thin skim coat of joint compound, or a product made for that purpose, over the entire board surface, not only the joints and fasteners. The skim coat evens out the difference between paper and compound so it does not show through gloss paint or under strong side lighting.

Does the finish level change how many sheets of drywall I need?

No. Board area and sheet count are the same at every level. Finish level changes joint compound, tape coats, sanding and finishing labor. That is why it belongs on the takeoff as its own area column instead of being folded into the sheet count.

Where do I find the finish level on a set of plans?

Usually in the project specifications under the gypsum board section, numbered 09 29 00 in MasterFormat, and sometimes in the room finish schedule, the general notes or the partition type sheet. If the drawings and the spec disagree, or neither states a level, send an RFI before you price it.

What finish level is used above a ceiling?

Areas hidden from view, such as walls above a suspended ceiling, are commonly Level 1, which is tape set in compound. On fire-rated or smoke-rated partitions the taping above the ceiling is part of the rated assembly, so follow the tested design and the spec, not a habit.

What finish level goes behind tile?

Level 2 is the level commonly specified where gypsum panels are a substrate for tile. Cement board and other backer units are a different product with their own joint treatment, so check which board the wall type calls for before you assign a level.

Is Level 5 required for every painted wall?

No. Level 4 is the common choice under flat paint and light texture. Level 5 is typically called for under gloss, semi-gloss and enamel paints and on surfaces that catch strong side lighting, such as long corridors with windows at the end or walls lit by wall washers. The spec decides, not the paint color alone.

Can takeoff software read the finish level for me?

It can give you the wall area that the levels are applied to. ReadMyBlueprint reads the wall-type legend and returns board area by wall type; the finish level usually lives in the spec book or finish schedule, so you assign levels to those areas yourself and confirm them against the documents.
