Why "98% accurate" should make you suspicious, not confident

Sep 10, 2026 · 5 min read

Almost every AI takeoff tool puts a single accuracy number on its homepage — 95%, 98%, sometimes higher. It reads like a guarantee. It isn't one, and it can't be, because a single percentage is structurally incapable of telling you the thing you actually need to know: which specific number in front of you right now is solid, and which one needs a second look.

What a blended percentage actually hides

An accuracy claim is almost always an average across some internal test set — a batch of plan sets the vendor chose, measured against some ground truth the vendor also chose. That's not dishonest on its own, but averaging hides exactly the information that matters on your project:

  • It doesn't tell you what was easy and what was hard. A clean, fully-labeled commercial set full of continuous straight walls raises the average. Your actual plan set — with a hand-annotated addendum sheet or an unusual wall type — might sit nowhere near that average.
  • It doesn't tell you which walls on your set it applies to. "98% accurate" says nothing about whether the 2% of misses landed on your CMU exterior or your interior partitions. An aggregate number can't localize to your project.
  • It's rarely independently verified. Most of these figures come from the vendor's own internal testing, on their own chosen benchmark. There's no industry-standard test every tool runs, so the numbers aren't even comparable to each other.

What a label on every measurement does instead

Instead of one number covering an entire plan set, a more useful signal is a label on each individual result, reflecting exactly how that specific number was produced:

3-5/8" 20-ga steel stud @ 16" o.c. — 312 studsMatched
8" CMU block — 4,180 blocksMatched
Unlabeled partition, 4.4" — 86 LFConfirm

The first two rows come from a wall whose measured thickness matched a spec that's actually printed on the wall-type legend — there's a real, checkable reason to trust that number. The third row is a wall with no matching spec anywhere in the document; the length is still exact, but the material can't be determined from the drawing alone, so it's flagged rather than filled in with a best guess.

That's a fundamentally different kind of information than "98% accurate overall." It tells you exactly where your two minutes of double-checking should go — not "trust the whole thing" or "distrust the whole thing," but "these three rows, specifically."

Why this is actually harder to fake: a blended accuracy percentage is one number you can pick before you ship the product. A confidence label on every row has to be right on that specific result, every time — there's no averaging away a wrong label on a wall someone's about to bid off of.

Why we don't publish an accuracy percentage either

This isn't a claim that ReadMyBlueprint is more accurate than any competitor's stated figure — there's no independent benchmark that every takeoff tool runs, so those numbers aren't comparable to begin with, and we're not going to invent one just to have a bigger number on the homepage. What we can tell you, and show you on every single result, is exactly how that number was produced: measured from real geometry and matched to a real spec, or flagged because it wasn't. That's a claim you can check yourself, on your own plan set, instead of one you have to take on faith.
