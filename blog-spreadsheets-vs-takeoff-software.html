Spreadsheets vs. takeoff software for drywall bids

By The ReadMyBlueprint Team · Oct 8, 2026 · 5 min read

The short answer: spreadsheets are excellent for pricing and weak for measuring. The workflow that holds up for most small drywall subs is a hybrid: get quantities from a takeoff tool, export them, and drop them into the pricing sheet you already trust.

What spreadsheets do well

  • Your own numbers. Labor units, waste factors, markups, and supplier pricing are yours, and a spreadsheet lets you set them exactly how you bid.
  • Flexibility. You can add a line for an odd condition (a curved soffit, a fire-rated shaft) without waiting for a vendor to support it.
  • Zero new software. Everyone already knows how to open one, and it costs nothing to start.
  • Transparency. Formulas are visible. Anyone can see how a total was built.

Where spreadsheets break

  • Quantities are typed in by hand. Whether you scale off paper or measure on a PDF, someone reads a length and types it. That's slow, and every typed number is a chance for a slip.
  • Formula errors hide. A broken range or a copied row can quietly change a total. The European Spreadsheet Risks Interest Group keeps a public collection of documented spreadsheet failures. The point isn't that your sheet is wrong, it's that you can't easily tell.
  • No link back to the drawing. A cell that says 1,240 LF doesn't tell you which walls it came from. When the GC asks about a number, you're re-measuring.
  • Version control. Addenda arrive, a copy gets saved as "final_v3", and two people edit different files. Which sheet matches which plan revision?
The pattern: spreadsheets fail at the step between the drawing and the cell. They're fine once the quantity is right.

What takeoff software changes

Takeoff software puts the measurement on the drawing itself. Manual tools give you running totals as you click; automated tools propose the quantities and ask you to review them. Either way the number is tied to a wall on a sheet, so you can find where it came from. What it usually doesn't do is replace your pricing logic, and in a small shop it shouldn't. A hybrid workflow that works

  1. Get quantities from the plans. Use a takeoff tool for studs, track, board and insulation counts, and add the one-off items (doors, specialty conditions) yourself.
  2. Review what's flagged. Confirm anything the tool wasn't sure about before it reaches a total.
  3. Export CSV or Excel. Keep the exported file as the record of what was measured, per revision.
  4. Paste into your pricing sheet. Your labor units, waste, and markup stay exactly as they are. Our drywall bid sheet post shows how to lay that sheet out.
  5. Sanity-check with a quick calculation. The drywall calculator and the guide on how to estimate drywall sheets are good cross-checks against the tool's totals.

When a spreadsheet alone is fine

Not every bid needs software. A single-room remodel, a repair job, or a quick change order is often faster with a tape, a calculator and a sheet. If you bid a handful of small jobs a month, the setup time of a takeoff tool may not be worth it. The tipping point is usually when plan sets get long enough that counting walls takes a night, or when you start bidding more jobs than you can measure by hand.

Habits that make the hybrid safer

  • Keep quantities and pricing on separate tabs. Paste the export into its own tab and have the pricing tab read from it. When an addendum lands, you replace one tab, not hunt through formulas.
  • Name files by plan revision. Put the date or addendum number in the export filename so the quantity record matches the drawings you bid from.
  • Carry the flags with you. If a quantity came out as unconfirmed, keep that note next to the row until someone resolves it.
  • Spot-check three walls. Pick a few and measure them yourself. If they agree, you've learned how much to trust the rest.

Quick answers

Is a spreadsheet good enough for drywall bids? For pricing, yes. For getting quantities off the plans, it relies on manual measuring and typing, which is where most errors and time loss happen.

Can I use takeoff software with my existing spreadsheet? Yes. Most takeoff tools export CSV or Excel, so quantities can go straight into your own pricing sheet.

What does ReadMyBlueprint export? A CSV or Excel results table with a column per material and a label column showing matched, ambiguous, or needs confirmation.

Does ReadMyBlueprint replace my bid sheet? No. It produces quantities for digital plan sets; your labor units, waste and markup stay in your own sheet.
